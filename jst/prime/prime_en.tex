% ==========================================
\begin{abstract}
This paper presents a cross-stage computational synthesis ($A \rightarrow D$) investigating the statistical and structural behavior of consecutive prime gaps $g_n = p_{n+1} - p_n$ up to $N = 10^9$. Using a strictly frozen protocol across four ascending stages ($10^6, 10^7, 10^8$, and $10^9$), we track the prime counting function $\pi(x)$, logarithmic integral deviations $D_1(x) = \pi(x) - \mathrm{Li}(x)$ and $D_2(x) = \pi(x) - x/\ln x$, local window densities $\rho_k$, and gap frequency rankings. Our empirical findings confirm that $D_1(x)$ remains negative at all closure checkpoints ($-1,699.912$ at $10^9$), local density decreases monotonically across the nine windows of Stage $D$ ($0.053175 \rightarrow 0.048383$), and the gap frequency ranking shifts in Stage $D$ where gap $10$ overtakes gaps $4$ and $2$. We refute the hypothesis of invariant frequency ranking, report a weak lag-1 correlation ($r = -0.03006$ in $M1$), and explicitly refrain from claiming any periodic or predictive law beyond the verified computational horizon.
\end{abstract}

% ==========================================
\section{Introduction, Problem Statement, and Hypothesis}
While prime numbers are deterministically defined, their local spacing exhibits intricate statistical behavior that raises fundamental questions regarding short-range memory and modular constraints.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Research Question:} Do consecutive prime gaps carry statistical ``memory'' or invariant structural patterns across increasing numerical scales?
    \item \textbf{Pre-Computation Null Hypothesis:} We initially hypothesized that gap sizes behave as statistically independent variables and that the relative frequency ranking of the most common gaps ($2, 4, 6, 10, 12, \dots$) remains invariant across stages $A, B, C$, and $D$.
    \item \textbf{Methodological Rule:} Every observation is recorded strictly if backed by a verified number in its respective stage. Interpretations are never recorded as universal laws, and uncomputed ranges are never extrapolated as proven facts.
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{Methodological note:} This paper presents an empirical observation and a tested hypothesis, not a theorem and not a conjecture. No claim is made beyond $10^9$.

\section{Definitions and Mathematical Protocol}

\subsection{The Edge (Prime Gap)}
An edge $g_n$ is defined as the arithmetic difference between two consecutive primes:
\[
g_n = p_{n+1} - p_n, \quad \forall n \geq 1
\]
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sieve Protocol:} Primes are generated via the Sieve of Eratosthenes up to $N$, with $1$ explicitly excluded from the prime set.
    \item \textbf{Unique Odd Edge:} The initial edge $g_1 = 3 - 2 = 1$ is the sole odd gap in the sequence. Every prime $p_n > 2$ is odd, making every subsequent gap strictly even ($g_n \equiv 0 \pmod 2$ for all $n \geq 2$).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Counting, Deviation, and Density Functions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\pi(x)$: Exact count of primes $\leq x$.
    \item $\mathrm{Li}(x) = \mathrm{Ei}(\ln x) - \mathrm{Ei}(\ln 2) = \int_2^x \frac{dt}{\ln t}$: Offset logarithmic integral.
    \item $D_1(x) = \pi(x) - \mathrm{Li}(x)$ and $D_2(x) = \pi(x) - x/\ln x$.
    \item $R(x) = |D_1(x)| / \pi(x)$: Relative logarithmic deviation.
    \item $\rho_k = (\pi(b_k) - \pi(a_k)) / 10^8$: Local prime density in window $k$. $\bar{g}_k = 1/\rho_k$ is the Estimated Mean Spacing in window $k$, derived from density; it is not the directly computed mean of the gaps in that window, which would be $\bar{g}_k^{\,\mathrm{actual}} = (1/m_k)\sum_{j=1}^{m_k} g_j$. The two values may differ slightly because of window boundaries and endpoint inclusion.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Stage $D$ Inclusion Rule and $M1$ Size Classes}
A gap $g_n = p_{n+1} - p_n$ is counted inside Stage $D$ if and only if its upper endpoint satisfies $p_{n+1} > 10^8$ ($100\text{M}$), yielding exactly $45,086,079$ gaps in Stage $D$. Gaps are partitioned into three exhaustive $M1$ classes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Small:} $g \leq 6$ ($\{1, 2, 4, 6\}$).
    \item \textbf{Medium:} $8 \leq g \leq 14$ ($\{8, 10, 12, 14\}$).
    \item \textbf{Large:} $g \geq 16$.
\end{itemize}
Because $g_1 = 1$ falls inside the Small class and all subsequent gaps are even, no odd integers ($7$ or $15$) exist between class boundaries.

\section{Cross-Stage Synthesis: Adjacency Structure and Primorial Sieve}
\subsection{Central Question}
Can the negative lag-1 autocorrelation of adjacent prime gaps ($r_1 < 0$) be derived directly from small-prime divisibility constraints, without assuming gap independence or a prior asymptotic law? This section is limited to the first $1,000,000$ primes ($p_{10^6} = 15,485,863$). No $N = 10,000,000$ computation is claimed, and no Hurst / DFA estimate is reported as proven.

\subsection{Adjacency Measures}
\[
r_k = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n-k} (g_i - \bar{g})(g_{i+k} - \bar{g})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (g_i - \bar{g})^2}
\qquad
R_{\text{stat}} = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^{n-1} \frac{\min(g_i, g_{i+1})}{\max(g_i, g_{i+1})}
\]
A shuffled control can be used to evaluate whether the observed lag-1 correlation is distinguishable from correlations generated by random reorderings that preserve the marginal gap distribution. The outcome of this control is not assumed in advance.

\subsection{Primorial Sieve Model}
For $M = \prod_{p \le p_k} p$, e.g. $M = 6, 30, 210, 2310, 30030, 510510$, the exact cycle of residues coprime to $M$ gives a closed gap cycle. For $M = 30$ ($\phi(30) = 8$):
\[
\mathcal{C}_{30} = [6, 4, 2, 4, 2, 4, 6, 2]
\]
These purely arithmetic cycles already have $r_{\text{sieve}} < 0$. These cycles show that adjacent-gap dependence can arise from modular constraints alone in a finite arithmetic model. This does not establish that the same mechanism causes the measured correlation in the actual prime sequence, and it does not prove a general asymptotic law.

\section{Extra-Memory Test of the Exact Gap Value (M1-E)}
The question is not only whether the current gap class is associated with the next gap class, but whether the exact numerical value of the current gap carries additional information beyond its size class alone.
Let the consecutive gap sequence be:
\[
(g_1,g_2,\ldots,g_m),
\]
and let \(S_n\in\{1,2,3\}\) be the class of gap \(g_n\), under the adopted classification:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{Small}&:\quad g\leq6,\\
\text{Medium}&:\quad 8\leq g\leq14,\\
\text{Large}&:\quad g\geq16.
\end{aligned}
\]
Model M1 tests the transition relation based on the previous class:
\[
P(S_{n+1}\mid S_n),
\]
while M1-E tests whether prediction can be improved using the exact value of the previous gap:
\[
P(S_{n+1}\mid g_n).
\]
Hence the central statistical question in M1-E is:
\[
\boxed{
\text{Does }g_n\text{ add predictive information beyond }
S_n\text{ when predicting }S_{n+1}?
}
\]
\paragraph{Test protocol.}
The M1-E protocol was fixed before evaluating the final result, and includes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Splitting transition data into training and test sets at \(50\%/50\%\).
    \item Representing exact gap values up to \(30\), and using fixed logarithmic bins for larger gaps.
    \item A merging threshold of \(200\), a fixed ascending merging system, with Laplace smoothing \(\alpha=1\).
    \item Block Bootstrap with block length \(100\), and \(B=2000\) repetitions.
    \item Evaluating improvement with Log-Loss, with the Pearson coefficient evaluated independently and the two judgments not merged into a single criterion.
    \item No change to the protocol after inspecting results.
\end{enumerate}
\paragraph{Implementation definitions (frozen).}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Split:} Chronological sequential split. The first half of the ordered transition sequence is the training set and the second half is the test set. Transitions are not randomly shuffled across the split, so that test evaluation is temporally independent of training and does not leak neighbouring transitions into training.
    \item \textbf{Pearson, defined:} The Pearson coefficient reported independently is the lag-1 correlation $r_1$ between consecutive class codes $S_n$ and $S_{n+1}$. It is a descriptive linear-correlation measure on the class sequence, computed separately from the Log-Loss comparison, and the two judgments are not merged into a single criterion. It is not a correlation between exact gap values and predicted probabilities, and it is not used to claim or deny memory in general.
    \item \textbf{Bootstrap, paired:} For uncertainty in the Log-Loss improvement, Block Bootstrap resamples blocks of length $100$ from the test transitions. The same resampled blocks are used for both M1 and M1-E in each repetition, so the comparison is paired on the same transitions. The confidence interval for the Log-Loss improvement $\Delta = \text{Log-Loss}_{M1} - \text{Log-Loss}_{M1-E}$ is obtained from the percentiles of the $B=2000$ paired bootstrap values of $\Delta$.
    \item \textbf{Seed, scoped:} \texttt{SEED = 20261120} is a fixed reproducibility setting for the stochastic part of the implementation, namely Block Bootstrap resampling and any tie-breaking. The chronological split itself is deterministic and is not decided by the seed. The seed alone does not prove the correctness of the result.
\end{enumerate}
\paragraph{Evaluation criterion.}
M1-E performance is measured on test data compared with model M1. A lower Log-Loss is interpreted as an improvement in predictive performance, with the magnitude of improvement and its uncertainty evaluated under the adopted test protocol. Absence of a clear improvement means no additional predictive evidence was found within the tested data and settings; it does not prove the absence of additional information in general.
\paragraph{Test status.}
The M1-E protocol is specified and frozen, but its final result on the actual prime-gap data has not yet been adopted. Therefore no simulation result or synthetic experimental data is presented as a result computed from the original prime sequence.
\subsection{Class-Order Test and Shuffled Control}
On the ordered class sequence, the first-order correlation coefficient was:
\[
r_1=-0.03006.
\]
This value indicates a weak negative linear correlation between the current gap class and the next gap class. To distinguish the effect of order from the effect of the marginal class distribution, a Shuffled Control test can be performed, reordering the sequence while preserving the original class frequencies.
The correlation in the original sequence is compared with the distribution of correlations from repeated shuffles. The test aims to assess whether the original adjacency order carries a statistical signal beyond what the class distribution alone can explain.
The negative correlation alone is not interpreted as proof of a specific causal mechanism, nor is the absence of a strong linear correlation evidence for the absence of all forms of dependence or memory.

\section{Discussion, Methodological Verification, and Limitations}
\subsection{What Changed vs. What Remained Invariant}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Invariants:} $D_1 < 0$ and $D_2 > 0$ held at every closure point from $A$ to $D$; gaps $6$ and $12$ retained Ranks $1$ and $2$ across all stages.
\item \textbf{Rank Shift in Stage $D$:} Up to Stage $C$, the cumulative top six gaps were ordered $(6, 12, 2, 4, 10, 18)$. Inside Stage $D$ alone, the ranking shifted to $(6, 12, 10, 4, 2, 18)$, with gap $10$ advancing to Rank $3$ and gaps $4$ and $2$ converging to within a difference of just $228$ occurrences ($2{,}984{,}422$ vs. $2{,}984{,}194$).
\item \textbf{Primorial Modular Constraint:} The finite primorial residue cycles provide an arithmetic example in which adjacent-gap dependence can arise from modular constraints alone. This observation motivates the investigation of adjacency structure in prime gaps, but it does not establish that the measured correlation in the actual prime sequence is caused solely by these constraints. The measured value in M1 is $r_1 = -0.03006$, a weak linear correlation, not a proof of repulsion or of a causal mechanism.
\end{itemize}
\subsection{Isolation of Verification Sieve Artifacts}
During independent verification of Stage $D$, a defective verification script that erroneously included $1$ as a prime nearly introduced an unwarranted adjustment. Isolating and discarding that artifact — strictly enforcing $1 \notin \mathbb{P}$ — was integral to validating the exact Stage $D$ gap count ($45{,}086{,}079$).
\subsection{Explicit Scope Boundaries (What Was Not Proven)}
Stages $A$--$D$ do \textbf{not} prove that $D_1(x)$ remains negative beyond $10^9$, nor do they establish any periodic law from $\Delta D_1$ fluctuations ($-750.4$ to $+732.688$), nor do they prove whether the near-tie between gaps $2$ and $4$ persists or reverses in Stage $E$ ($1\text{B} \rightarrow 10\text{B}$).

\section{Committee Questions}
\textbf{Why exclude 1?} Because $1$ is not prime; including it creates a false gap.

\textbf{Why is gap 1 the only odd gap?} After $2$, all primes are odd, and the difference of two odds is even.

\textbf{Can you predict the next gap?} No, $r = -0.03006$ is too weak for prediction.

\textbf{Did you claim a general law?} No, observation up to $10^9$ only, no theorem, no conjecture.

\subsection*{Extra-Memory Test Protocol (M1-E) --- Summary for the Committee}
M1-E tests a sharper question than class-order testing: does the exact value $g_n$ add predictive information beyond class $S_n$ when predicting $S_{n+1}$? That is, comparing $P(S_{n+1}\mid S_n)$ in M1 with $P(S_{n+1}\mid g_n)$ in M1-E. The protocol is frozen before evaluating the result (chronological 50\%/50\% split, first half training, second half test, exact representation up to $30$, merging threshold $200$, Laplace smoothing $\alpha=1$, paired Block Bootstrap with block length $100$ and $B=2000$, evaluation by Log-Loss, and $r_1$ between $S_n$ and $S_{n+1}$ evaluated independently). The final result on actual prime data has not yet been adopted, and no simulation is presented as a result computed from the original sequence.
\textbf{How does M1-E differ from the shuffled control?} The shuffled control is only an auxiliary test of order effect; it preserves the class distribution and breaks its order. Alone, it does not prove an exclusive cause, and it does not substitute for the exact-value test.
\textbf{Why did you choose the seed \texttt{SEED = 20261120} specifically in the M1-E test?} To ensure strict reproducibility and to prevent any biased selection of results (cherry-picking); the seed is derived from the protocol freeze date before execution (20 November 2026). The seed governs only the stochastic part (Bootstrap resampling and tie-breaking); the chronological split is deterministic and is not decided by the seed. The seed alone does not prove the correctness of the result.
\textbf{Did $r_1=-0.03006$ prove repulsion or the absence of memory?} No. It is a weak negative linear correlation observed on the class sequence, conditional on the correctness of the calculation. Pearson does not measure all forms of dependence, does not prove a specific causal mechanism, and does not rule out memory in general. M1-E was designed precisely to test whether the exact value adds information that the classes did not reveal.

\section{Reproducibility and Conclusion}
All prime-generation and counting computations reported in Stages $A$--$D$ were performed in \texttt{Python 3} using the Sieve of Eratosthenes, with $1$ excluded from the prime set. The M1-E protocol and its reproducibility settings have been frozen, but the final M1-E evaluation on the actual prime-gap dataset remains pending. While M1 revealed no strong linear predictive power in the test performed ($r_1 = -0.03006$), and the memory question is not settled before M1-E is completed, their frequency hierarchy undergoes measurable structural reorganization driven by decreasing local density.
