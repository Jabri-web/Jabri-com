5.0. Problem Statement

This frame aims to study the Spectral Gap of a weighted differential operator on the open interval \((0, L)\), where \(L = 2\pi\). We start from a weighted form space and apply the Ground-State Transformation to factor out the principal component, which transforms the problem into a new operator with weight \(w(u)\). The central question is: Does there exist a positive spectral gap \(\lambda_1 > 0\) for the weighted operator, and what is the certified lower bound for it?

5.0.1. Conceptual Note: Why is the Gap "Weighted"?

We call it the weighted spectral gap because the weight enters the definition of the function space, the energy, and the Laplace operator itself. The pivotal step was applying the Ground-State Transformation by substituting \(f = g \cdot \psi\). This transformation completely eliminated the potential term \(V\), leaving us with a simplified quadratic form:

\[ \mathcal{Q}[f] = \int w(u) |\psi'(u)|^2 du \]

The weight was born from the square of the ground state: \(w(u) = g^2(u)\). Here, the weight is no longer just a numerical factor; it becomes a Weight Density that defines the measure, integration, and weighted energy. The new space \(L^2(0, 2\pi; w\,du)\) possesses a distinct geometry, and the operator \(H_w = -\frac{1}{w}(w\psi')'\) is the natural operator within this weighted space.

5.1. Initial Form Space

We define the base function \(g(u)\) on the open interval \((0, 2\pi)\):

\[ g(u) = \left(\frac{u}{2\pi}\right)^5 \log\left(\frac{2\pi}{u}\right) \exp\left(-\frac{2\pi}{21u}\right) \]

We define the weight \(w(u) = g^2(u)\). The initial form space is:

\[ H^1_w(0,L) = \left\{ \psi \in AC_{\mathrm{loc}}(0,L) : \int_0^L w(u)(|\psi|^2+|\psi'|^2)\,du < \infty \right\} \]
Warning: The definition of \(H^1_w\) alone does not determine the closure of the form nor the associated self-adjoint extension. Identifying the intended domain for the problem with natural Neumann conditions requires proving the closedness of the form and completeness of the space in the energy norm, then applying the representation theorem for closed forms. This step is not yet complete.

5.2. Ground-State Transformation

For each function \(f = g\psi\), we define the differential operator \(A\):

\[ p(u) = \frac{g'(u)}{g(u)}, \qquad A = -\frac{d}{du} + p(u) \]

Applying the operator to \(f = g\psi\):

\[ Af = -g'\psi - g\psi' + p g\psi = -g\psi' \]

Thus, the quadratic form \(\mathcal{Q}_F\) becomes:

\[ \mathcal{Q}_F[f] = \int_0^{2\pi} |Af|^2 du = \int_0^{2\pi} w(u) |\psi'(u)|^2 du \]

And the orthogonality condition with the ground state \(g\) becomes:

\[ \int_0^{2\pi} w(u)\psi(u) \, du = 0 \]
Note: This algebraic derivation is valid inside the open interval. Determining the operational domain and boundary conditions remains an independent step.

5.3. Weighted Differential Expression

The quadratic form \(\mathcal{E}[\psi] = \int w |\psi'|^2 du\) is associated with the formal differential expression:

\[ H_w \psi = -\frac{1}{w} (w \psi')' \]

"This is the formal differential expression associated with the quadratic form. The choice of self-adjoint realization and the determination of its operational domain still require independent proof."

Status of identification: The differential expression above is formal until the operator domain is specified and the form is proven to be closed and densely defined, then applying the representation theorem for closed forms to obtain the associated self-adjoint extension.

5.5. Tail Asymptotic Analysis near Zero

Approved Status: Proven analytically (rigorous \(C_0 > 0\)).

To determine the sign of \(B_-'\) near zero, we reformulate via:

\[ M(x) = \int_0^x w(u)\,du, \quad R(x) = \int_x^m \frac{du}{w(u)}, \quad B_-(x) = M(x)R(x) \]

Since \(w > 0\), the sign of \(B_-'(x)\) equals the sign of \(S(x)\):

\[ h(x) := w(x)^2 R(x) - M(x), \quad h'(x) = 2w(x) S(x) \]

Where \(S(x) = w'(x)R(x) - 1\). To prove \(S(x) > 0\) near zero, we use integration by parts rigorously.

Rigorous Integration by Parts and \(T_0\) Verification

Let \(\alpha = a/m\) and \(T_0 = \max\{\alpha, ae/L\}\). From the given values:

\[ L = 2\pi \approx 6.283185,\quad a = \frac{4\pi}{21} \approx 0.598399 \] \[ \frac{ae}{L} = \frac{0.598399 \times 2.71828}{6.283185} \approx 0.2589 \] \[ \alpha = \frac{a}{m} \approx \frac{0.598399}{4.941} \approx 0.1211 < 0.2589 \] \[ \therefore T_0 = \frac{ae}{L} \approx 0.2589 < 6 \]

Precise Definition of \(C_0\)

Applying integration by parts twice on \([T_0, T]\):

\[ I(T) = I(T_0) + e^T(q_0(T) - q_0'(T)) - e^{T_0}(q_0(T_0) - q_0'(T_0)) + \int_{T_0}^T e^t q_0''(t)\,dt \]

Where \(I(T_0) = \int_{\alpha}^{T_0} e^t q_0(t) dt\). Define the constant:

\[ \boxed{C_0 = I(T_0) - e^{T_0}(q_0(T_0) - q_0'(T_0))} \]

Since \(T_0 = ae/L \approx 0.2589 < 6\) and \(c(T_0) = 8 - 2/\log(L T_0/a) = 8 - 2/\log(e) = 6\):

\[ 1 - \frac{c(T_0)}{T_0} = 1 - \frac{6}{0.2589} < 0 \] \[ \therefore -e^{T_0}(q_0(T_0) - q_0'(T_0)) > 0 \]

And since \(I(T_0) > 0\), we get:

\[ \boxed{C_0 > 0} \]

Exact Formula for \(S(x)\) and Final Result

Defining \(E(T) = \frac{C_0 + \int_{T_0}^T e^t q_0''(t) dt}{e^T q_0(T)} > 0\), we get the exact formula:

\[ S(x) = \frac{2}{T} - \frac{c(c+2)}{T^2} + \left(1 + \frac{c+2}{T}\right)E(T) \]

Since \(E(T) > 0\):

\[ S(x) > \frac{2}{T} - \frac{c(c+2)}{T^2} \]

To guarantee \(S(x) > 0\), we need \(2T > c(c+2)\). Since \(c < 8\), \(c(c+2) < 80\). Thus, \(T > 40\) suffices:

\[ \boxed{ 0 < x < \frac{a}{40} \approx 0.015 \implies h'(x) > 0 } \]
Result: Since \(h'(x) > 0\) on \((0, 0.015]\), and \(h(0) = 0\), it follows that \(h(x) > 0\) on this domain. The tail at zero is closed analytically and rigorously, with no imported constants.

5.10. Diagnostic Numerical Reference: Weighted Median

Approved Status: Diagnostic Numerical Reference — NOT a certificate.

5.10.1. Definition of the Weighted Median

The weighted median \(m\) of the weight \(w(u)\) on the interval \((0, L)\) is defined by the mass equation:

\[ \int_0^{m} w(u)\,du \;=\; \int_{m}^{L} w(u)\,du \;=\; \frac{M(L)}{2}, \qquad M(x) := \int_0^{x} w(u)\,du. \]

This is a mass equality, not an equality of lengths. Therefore, \(m\) is not, in general, the midpoint \(L/2\). Any confusion between the two concepts corrupts all subsequent bounds.

5.10.3. Proof that \(F(L/2) \neq 0\) — The Median is Not the Midpoint

The weight \(w(u)\) is not symmetric about \(L/2\), because the left endpoint vanishes exponentially and the right endpoint vanishes quadratically:

\[ w(u) \sim u^{10}\log^2(L/u)\,e^{-a/u}\ \ (u \to 0^+), \qquad w(u) \sim \left(\frac{L-u}{L}\right)^{2}\ \ (u \to L^-). \]

But asymmetry alone does not prove that the median \(m\) moves away from \(L/2\); the two masses could still balance at the midpoint despite asymmetry. The direct argument is to prove:

\[ F(L/2) \;=\; 2\,M(L/2) - M(L) \;\neq\; 0. \]

Interval Proof

5.11.1. Definition and Goal

Define the auxiliary function:

\[ F(x) \;=\; 2\,M(x) - M(L), \qquad M(x) := \int_0^{x} w(u)\,du. \]

The weighted median \(m\) satisfies \(F(m) = 0\) (since \(M(m) = M(L)/2\)). The goal: to prove that \(m \neq L/2\), i.e., \(F(L/2) \neq 0\).

5.11.2. Diagnostic Interval Computation

Using a geometric partition to enclose \(M(L/2)\) and \(M(L)\), with the tail \((0, x_0]\) closed analytically and the tail near \(L\) closed via a triple partition (increasing / peak / decreasing), we obtained:

\[ F(L/2) \;\in\; [\,-8.1682\times10^{-3},\ -7.9078\times10^{-3}\,]. \]

Since the entire interval is strictly negative:

\[ \boxed{\,F(L/2) < 0\,} \]

And by continuity of \(F\) (since \(M\) is the integral of a continuous function), and since the median satisfies \(F(m) = 0\), we have:

\[ \boxed{\,m \neq L/2\,} \]

5.11.4. Interpretation

The weighted median is determined by accumulated mass, not geometric distance. The weighted mass is distributed unequally on \((0, L)\), and the median lies to the right of the midpoint. It does not follow that the weight \(w(u)\) is increasing on the entire interval; it reaches a peak at \(u \approx 5.156\) then decreases. What is the Magnitude of the Spectral Gap in Frame 5?

Here we arrive at a very important question: What do we actually possess regarding the spectral gap, what are its mathematical limits, and where does it stand compared to the Navier gap? I will distinguish between the magnitude, the field, and the domain, followed by the comparison.

1. What is the magnitude of the spectral gap in Frame 5?

We have: \(L=2\pi\), and \(a=\frac{4\pi}{21}\). The weight is:

\[ w(u) = \left(\frac{u}{L}\right)^{10} \log^2\left(\frac{L}{u}\right) e^{-a/u} \]

And the operator is: \(H_w\psi = -\frac{1}{w}(w\psi')'\). The intended gap is the first positive spectral value after the zero mode (constant).

We have three numbers that must not be confused:

QuantityValueSignificance
Numerical Candidate (Neumann)1.7685Numerically converged Neumann/Mean-Zero candidate.
Truncated Dirichlet Problem~2.0794FEM result for a different problem (Dirichlet), not the intended problem.
Raw Muckenhoupt Estimate0.8116Raw analytical estimate, not a certified bound.

2. Have we identified the edges of the gap?

Yes, we identified the boundaries of the mathematical domain: \(\boxed{I=(0,2\pi)}\). But note the nature of the two endpoints:

  • Left endpoint (exponentially degenerate): \(u\to0^+\), \(w(u)\to0\) due to the factor \(e^{-a/u}\).
  • Right endpoint (quadratically degenerate): \(u\to2\pi^-\), \(w(u)\sim \frac{e^{-a/L}}{L^2}(L-u)^2\) where \(L=2\pi\) and \(a=4\pi/21\).

Thus, we have an open domain with two degenerate endpoints. Identifying the domain endpoints does not mean we have numerically identified the edges of the spectral gap.

3. Energy Space and Closure Issue

In the current formulation, the energy space is referred to as the "closure of smooth functions", but this is not yet resolved. The choice of closure, the operational domain, and proving the operator is self-adjoint remain open problems in this frame. The Hilbert space \(L^2(0, 2\pi; w\,du)\) is clear, but the definition of the energy space \(H^1_w\) requires final adjustment.

4. Where do we stand scientifically?

I see that we have a mathematically well-defined project:

  • The domain, weight, operator, zero mode, and the formulation of the gap are known.
  • We have numerical calculations for the truncated domain, and partial interval enclosures.
  • The guaranteed quantitative certificate, the global interval comparison, and self-adjointness are still issues that need closure.

And here is the question worth working on: Can we prove that the two degenerate endpoints do not allow a continuous spectrum to emerge approaching zero, and that the first positive value remains isolated from it? This is the central question in Frame 5.
