5.0. Problem Statement

This frame aims to study the Spectral Gap of a weighted differential operator on the open interval \((0, L)\), where \(L = 2\pi\).

We start from a weighted form space and apply the Ground-State Transformation to factor out the principal component, which transforms the problem into a new operator with weight $w(u)$. The central question is:

Does there exist a positive spectral gap \(\lambda_1 > 0\) for the weighted operator, and what is the certified lower bound for it?

To solve this, we study the function \(B(x)\) associated with the weighted Hardy constant. Proving a global upper bound for \(B(x)\), along with verifying the applicability of the weighted Hardy inequality to the adopted form space and its domain, provides a certified lower bound for the spectral gap. The mathematical journey in this frame is the journey to close this proof: from numerical evidence to rigorous analytical certification.

5.0.1. Conceptual Note: Why is the Gap "Weighted"?

We call it the weighted spectral gap because the weight enters the definition of the function space, the energy, and the Laplace operator itself, and not merely as a descriptive label for the gap.

Initially, we had a standard spectral problem for a differential operator. The pivotal step was applying the Ground-State Transformation by substituting \(f = g \cdot \psi\). This transformation completely eliminated the potential term \(V\) (thanks to integration by parts and the properties of the ground state \(g\)), leaving us with a simplified quadratic form:

\[ \mathcal{Q}[f] = \int w(u) |\psi'(u)|^2 du \]

The weight was thus born from the square of the ground state: \(w(u) = g^2(u)\). Here, the weight is no longer just a numerical factor; it becomes a Metric that redefines how mass, variance, and energy are measured. The new space \(L^2(0, 2\pi; w\,du)\) possesses a distinct geometry, and the operator \(H_w = -\frac{1}{w}(w\psi')'\) is the natural operator within this weighted space, rather than a standard Laplacian. This mathematical structure is what makes Frame 5 an independent study, rather than a mere numerical computation of a conventional spectral gap.

5.1. Initial Form Space

We define the base function $g(u)$ on the open interval $(0, 2\pi)$:

\[ g(u) = \left(\frac{u}{2\pi}\right)^5 \log\left(\frac{2\pi}{u}\right) \exp\left(-\frac{2\pi}{21u}\right) \]

We define the weight $w(u) = g^2(u)$. The initial form space is:

\[ H^1_w(0,L) = \left\{ \psi \in AC_{\mathrm{loc}}(0,L) : \int_0^L w(u)(|\psi|^2+|\psi'|^2)\,du < \infty \right\} \]
Warning: The definition of $H^1_w$ alone does not determine the closure of the form nor the associated self-adjoint extension. Identifying the intended domain for the problem with natural Neumann conditions requires proving the closedness of the form and completeness of the space in the energy norm, then applying the representation theorem for closed forms. 5.2. Ground-State Transformation

For each function $f = g\psi$, we define the differential operator $A$:

\[ p(u) = \frac{g'(u)}{g(u)}, \qquad A = -\frac{d}{du} + p(u) \]

Applying the operator to $f = g\psi$:

\[ Af = -g'\psi - g\psi' + p g\psi = -g\psi' \]

Thus, the quadratic form $\mathcal{Q}_F$ becomes:

\[ \mathcal{Q}_F[f] = \int_0^{2\pi} |Af|^2 du = \int_0^{2\pi} w(u) |\psi'(u)|^2 du \]

And the orthogonality condition with the ground state $g$ becomes:

\[ \int_0^{2\pi} w(u)\psi(u) \, du = 0 \]
Note: This algebraic derivation is valid inside the open interval. Determining the operational domain and boundary conditions remains an independent step.

5.3. Self-Adjoint Operator

The quadratic form $\mathcal{E}[\psi] = \int w |\psi'|^2 du$ is associated with the formal differential expression:

\[ H_w \psi = -\frac{1}{w} (w \psi')' \]

5.5. Asymptotic Analysis of $h(x)$ near Zero

To determine the sign of the derivative of $B_-$ near zero, we reformulate the problem via the auxiliary function:

\[ M(x) = \int_0^x w(u)\,du, \quad R(x) = \int_x^m \frac{du}{w(u)}, \quad B_-(x) = M(x)R(x) \]

Since $w > 0$, the sign of the derivative $B_-'(x)$ equals the sign of:

\[ h(x) := w(x)^2 R(x) - M(x), \quad \text{where } w(x)B_-'(x) = h(x) \]

Using integration by parts twice, we algebraically extract the leading term:

\[ h(x) = \frac{4}{L^{10}a^2}\, x^{13}\,\ell(x)^2\, e^{-a/x} + \mathcal{R}(x) \]

Where $\ell(x) = \log(L/x)$, \(L = 2\pi\), \(a = 4\pi/21\). The remainder \(\mathcal{R}(x)\) is bounded as:

\[ |\mathcal{R}(x)| \le (C_A + C_B) x^{14}\ell(x)^2 e^{-a/x} \]
Correction of \(R_{A1}\) Enclosure (without false assumptions):

Using the substitution \(s = x/u\) (where \(s \ge 2\) on \([0, x/2]\)):

\[ J_{A1} = x^{13} e^{-a/x} \int_2^\infty s^{-14} Q(\ell + \ln s) e^{-\frac{a}{x}(s-1)} ds \]

Since \(\ell + \ln s \le \ell s\), we have \(Q \le 208\ell^2 s^2\), therefore:

\[ J_{A1} \le \frac{208}{2^{12} a} x^{14} \ell^2 e^{-a/x} \implies R_{A1} \le \frac{208}{2^{12} a^3} x^{14} \ell^2 e^{-a/x} \]

Thus, the corrected constant \(C_A^*\) is:

\[ C_A^* = 208 + \frac{13}{2^{10}}(1+\ln2)^2 + 1612(1+\ln2)^2 + \frac{208}{2^{12}} \]
Derivation of \(\varepsilon_m\) from the Original Integral:

Applying integration by parts twice on \(R(x)\), the boundary term at \(m\) appears:

\[ \varepsilon_m = -\frac{m^{-8}\ell^{-2}e^{a/m}}{a} + \frac{m^{-7}\ell^{-2}(8 - 2/\ell)e^{a/m}}{a^2} \]

What is the Magnitude of the Spectral Gap in Frame 5?

Here we arrive at a very important question: What do we actually possess regarding the spectral gap, what are its mathematical limits, and where does it stand compared to the Navier gap? I will distinguish between the magnitude, the field, and the domain, followed by the comparison.

1. What is the magnitude of the spectral gap in Frame 5?

We have: \(L=2\pi\), and \(a=\frac{4\pi}{21}\). The weight is:

\[ w(u) = \left(\frac{u}{L}\right)^{10} \log^2\left(\frac{L}{u}\right) e^{-a/u} \]

And the operator is: \(H_w\psi = -\frac{1}{w}(w\psi')'\). The intended gap is the first positive spectral value after the zero mode (constant):

\[ \lambda_* = \inf_{\substack{\int_0^L \psi w \, du = 0 \\ \psi \ne 0}} \frac{\int_0^L |\psi'|^2 w \, du}{\int_0^L |\psi|^2 w \, du} \]

We have three numbers that must not be confused:

Thus, we have not yet determined the exact value of the gap. Moreover, the bound has not yet become a certified guarantee; because calculating \(B\) requires analytical control and certified error bounds. As for the value 2.0794, it cannot be attributed to the problem on the open domain before proving the convergence of the truncated problems.

2. Have we identified the edges of the gap?

Yes, we identified the boundaries of the mathematical domain: \(\boxed{I=(0,2\pi)}\). But note the nature of the two endpoints:

  • Left endpoint (exponentially degenerate): \(u\to0^+\), \(w(u)\to0\) due to the factor \(e^{-a/u}\).
  • Right endpoint (quadratically degenerate): \(u\to2\pi^-\), \(w(u)\sim C(2\pi-u)^2\) where \(C>0\).

Thus, we have an open domain with two degenerate endpoints, not a standard box with conventional walls. Most importantly: identifying the domain endpoints does not mean we have numerically identified the edges of the spectral gap. The gap is the distance between the zero eigenvalue and the first positive value, not the geometric distance between the domain endpoints.

3. What is the field and domain of the gap?

We have three levels:

The energy space can be defined as the closure of smooth functions under the norm: \(\|\psi\|_{H^1_w}^2 = \int_I (|\psi|^2+|\psi'|^2)w\,du\). With the necessity to specify the choice of closure and the self-adjointness of the operator at the endpoints. The gap pertains to the subspace with zero weighted mean: \(L^2_0(I,w\,du) = \{\psi: \int_I \psi w\,du=0\}\).
